How the MSM Rating Works

A raw time only tells you how fast someone ran — not how hard it was. The MSM Rating turns every result into one comparable number by adjusting for the course, the field around the runner, and the weather. Here's exactly how, math and all.

TL;DR The short version

We convert each race to a pace (seconds per meter), then divide that pace by a small adjustment that captures how much the conditions helped or hurt. A tougher course, running alone with nobody to push you, or bad weather all make an equal time more impressive, so they raise the rating. The adjusted pace is mapped onto a 1000-point scale where higher is better.

rating = 1000 + 3500 × ( REF − adjustedPace ) / REF

where adjustedPace = rawPace ÷ (courseFactor × fieldFactor × weatherFactor), and REF is the pace of a benchmark performance (16:00 for a 5K boys race, 18:30 girls).

0 Reading the number

1000
The reference performance — a 16:00 boys / 18:30 girls 5K on a neutral course in an average field.
+35
Roughly 1% faster than the runner one step below, on a neutral course.
Higher
Always better. The rating already accounts for conditions, so you can compare two runners who never raced each other.

Because everything is normalized to a neutral course and average field, an 1150 at a flat, fast invitational and an 1150 on a brutal state-meet course represent the same underlying fitness — which is the entire point.

1 Start with pace, not time

Times aren't comparable across distances — a 5K and a 3200 can't be lined up directly. So the first move is to reduce every result to seconds per meter:

rawPace = time_in_seconds ÷ distance_in_meters

All the adjustments below act on pace, and the final rating is distance-independent. That's why the rankings can mix a 4K, a 5K, and an 8K and still rank them fairly.

2 Course difficulty

Some courses are simply slower: hills, mud, tight turns, thick grass. A time on a hard course deserves more credit. We estimate each course's difficulty two independent ways and blend them.

A. Terrain from the map (GPX)

When a course has a GPS track, we walk its points, sum the elevation gained, and divide by the distance to get climb-per-kilometer. More climbing per km ⇒ a higher factor. About 15 m/km is treated as neutral WV terrain.

hillFactor = 1 + (climbPerKm − 15) × 0.004 (clamped 0.94–1.14)

If there's no GPS track, we fall back to the admin difficulty label (easy 0.96 · medium 1.00 · hard 1.06).

B. The runners themselves (common-athlete calibration)

The map doesn't know about footing, heat traps, or a course that "just runs slow." So we also let the athletes tell us. The idea: a course is hard if the same runners run slower on it than they do everywhere else.

For every athlete who has run a given course and at least one other course, we compute the ratio of their pace on that course to their median pace elsewhere. Average those ratios across all such runners and you get a difficulty measured purely from performance:

commonFactor(course) = median over shared athletes of ( their pace ON this course ) ÷ ( their median pace on OTHER courses )

C. Blend, weighted by evidence

A course run by 40 athletes has trustworthy common-athlete data; one run by three doesn't. So we blend the two, letting the data term earn trust as the number of shared athletes grows (capped at 70% weight):

w = min(commonAthletes ÷ 12, 0.70) courseFactor = hillFactor × (1 − w) + commonFactor × w (clamped 0.90–1.14)
A rarely-run course leans on its terrain map; a heavily-run course is calibrated by how real athletes actually performed on it. Over a season, the more a course is used, the sharper its rating gets.

3 Field strength — now individualized

This is the biggest change, and it comes straight from how racing actually feels. Two runners with the same time did not necessarily do the same amount of work.

  • Running alone — way ahead of everyone, or stuck in no-man's-land — is harder. There's nobody to draft, nobody to chase, no rhythm to lock onto. An equal time run solo reflects more fitness, so it earns a boost.
  • Running inside a tight, fast pack is easier for the same clock time — you get pulled along and carried through the middle miles. That earns a small discount.

The old model gave everyone in a race the same field factor based on the median time. The new model looks at each runner's own neighborhood in the results.

How the pack is measured

Within a race (same meet, gender, distance), we sort finishers by time and look at each runner's three nearest neighbors ahead and three behind. We compute the average pace gap to those neighbors, then compare it to the race's typical spacing (the median gap between consecutive finishers). That gives an isolation score:

isolation = 0.4 × (relative gap to runners AHEAD) + 0.6 × (relative gap to runners BEHIND)

The gap to runners behind is weighted more heavily — leading a race with a big cushion is the textbook "ran alone" case. An isolation near 1 means normal spacing (no change); above 1 means isolated (boost); below 1 means tightly packed (discount).

fieldFactor = 1 − 0.03 × tanh( (isolation − 1) × 0.6 ) (clamped 0.97–1.03)
Why capped at ±3%? Pack effects are real but modest — they nudge, they don't dominate. Course and weather can move a rating more. The tanh keeps extreme gaps from producing runaway adjustments: a runner who wins by a mile gets a firm-but-bounded boost, not an absurd one.
Example. Two girls both run 19:10. One won by 45 seconds over an empty course; the other was 5th in a dense pack where the top 8 finished within 12 seconds. The solo winner ran the harder race for that time — so she comes out with the higher MSM Rating, even though the clock says they tied.

4 Weather

Using each meet's date and location, we pull the historical race-day conditions (temperature, wind, precipitation) and build a small difficulty factor. Heat is the biggest penalty; wind and rain add a little.

ConditionEffect on factor
Hot (> 18 °C)up to +9%, scaling with heat
Very cold (< −5 °C)up to +4%
Windy (> 15 km/h)up to +5%
Wet (> 1 mm precip)up to +6%

If a meet's weather can't be retrieved, the factor is simply 1.0 (neutral) — weather never invents an adjustment it can't support.

5 Putting it together

The three factors multiply into a single boost, which is clamped so no single race can be over-corrected, then divides the raw pace:

boost = courseFactor × fieldFactor × weatherFactor (clamped 0.90–1.10) adjustedPace = rawPace ÷ boost rating = round( 1000 + 3500 × (REF − adjustedPace) ÷ REF )

A boost above 1 means conditions were hard (tough course, solo effort, bad weather) → the pace is treated as faster than the clock → a higher rating. A boost below 1 means conditions helped → the rating is trimmed. The ±10% overall clamp is the guardrail: even a perfect storm of adjustments can't swing a rating by more than a tenth.

Projected 5K. Any rating can be turned back into an equivalent neutral-course time at any distance — that's the "proj 5K" you see next to each runner. It's the rating formula run in reverse.

6 What the rating actually means

It answers one question: "How good was this performance, once you strip away the things the runner didn't control?" Because every result lands on the same neutral scale, you can:

Compare across meets

Rank runners who never toed the same line, on different courses, in different weather.

Compare across distances

A strong 3200 and a strong 5K sit on one list, since everything is pace-based.

Track real progress

A rising rating means genuine improvement — not just an easier course or a tailwind.

Reward hard efforts

Solo front-running and brutal courses finally get the credit the stopwatch hides.

7 Honest limits

  • It's a model, not a verdict. The factors are principled estimates, deliberately clamped so they stay conservative. On any given day, a race can defy them.
  • Data-hungry. Course calibration needs runners who've raced multiple courses; field adjustment needs a real field. Tiny races and one-off courses lean on the terrain map and get less individualized treatment.
  • Weather is daily, not hourly. We use the day's conditions at the venue, not the exact gun-time microclimate.
  • Same engine everywhere. The rankings page and the athlete/coach portal now run this identical code, so a rating is the same number wherever you see it.
