How the MSM Rating Works

A raw time only tells you how fast someone ran — not how hard it was. The MSM Rating turns every result into one comparable number by normalizing for distance and then adjusting for the course, the field around the runner, and the weather. Here's exactly how, math and all.

TL;DR The short version

We first convert every result to an equivalent 5K pace so a mile, a 3200, and a 5K can be compared fairly. Then we divide that pace by a small boost that captures how much the conditions helped or hurt. A tougher course, running alone with nobody to push you, or bad weather all make an equal effort more impressive, so they raise the rating. The adjusted pace is mapped onto a 1000-point scale where higher is better.

rating = round( 1000 + 3500 × ( REF − adjustedPace ) / REF )

where adjustedPace = pace5K ÷ (courseFactor × fieldFactor × weatherFactor), pace5K is the Riegel-normalized 5K pace, and REF is the pace of a benchmark performance (16:00 for boys, 18:30 for girls).

Because everything is normalized to a neutral course and average field, an 1150 at a flat, fast invitational and an 1150 on a brutal state-meet course represent the same underlying fitness — which is the entire point.

1 Normalize the distance first

Times aren't comparable across distances, and neither is raw pace — a miler holds a much faster pace than they could for 5K. Straight pace extrapolation would badly over-credit short races. So we convert every result to an equivalent 5K using the Riegel endurance model, then take that as a 5K pace:

equiv5K = time × (5000 / distance)^1.06 pace5K = equiv5K / 5000

The exponent 1.06 is the standard endurance fatigue factor: doubling the distance costs more than double the time. A 4:45 mile projects to a realistic 5K, not an impossibly fast one, so short races no longer dominate the leaderboard. Every adjustment below acts on this pace5K, which is why the rankings can mix a 4K, a 5K, and an 8K and still rank them fairly.

Riegel is why a blazing 1600 and a strong 5K land on one honest list. Without it, every mile time would look like a national-class 5K.

2 Course difficulty

Some courses are simply slower: hills, mud, tight turns, thick grass. A time on a hard course deserves more credit. We estimate each course's difficulty two independent ways and blend them.

A. Terrain from the map (GPX)

When a course has a stored climb-per-kilometer figure (from its GPS track), more climbing per km ⇒ a higher factor. About 15 m/km is treated as neutral WV terrain.

hillFactor = 1 + (climbPerKm − 15) × 0.004 (clamped 0.94–1.14)

If there's no GPS data, we fall back to the admin difficulty label (easy 0.96 · medium 1.00 · hard 1.06), and if there's neither, the terrain factor is a neutral 1.00.

B. The runners themselves (common-athlete calibration)

The map doesn't know about footing, heat traps, or a course that "just runs slow." So we also let the athletes tell us. The idea: a course is hard if the same runners run slower on it than they do everywhere else.

For every athlete who has run a given course and at least one other course, we compare their 5K-equivalent pace on that course to their median pace elsewhere. Take the median of those ratios across all such runners and you get a difficulty measured purely from performance:

commonFactor(course) = median over shared athletes of ( their pace5K ON this course ) ÷ ( their median pace5K on OTHER courses )

C. Blend, weighted by evidence

A course run by 40 athletes has trustworthy common-athlete data; one run by three doesn't. So we blend the two, letting the data term earn trust as the number of shared athletes grows (capped at 70% weight):

w = min(sharedAthletes ÷ 12, 0.70) courseFactor = hillFactor × (1 − w) + commonFactor × w (clamped 0.90–1.14)
A rarely-run course leans on its terrain map; a heavily-run course is calibrated by how real athletes actually performed on it. Over a season, the more a course is used, the sharper its rating gets.

3 Field strength — individualized

This comes straight from how racing actually feels. Two runners with the same time did not necessarily do the same amount of work.

  • Running alone — way ahead of everyone, or stuck in no-man's-land — is harder. There's nobody to draft, nobody to chase, no rhythm to lock onto. An equal time run solo reflects more fitness, so it earns a boost.
  • Running inside a tight, fast pack is easier for the same clock time — you get pulled along and carried through the middle miles. That earns a small discount.

Rather than give everyone in a race the same field factor, the model looks at each runner's own neighborhood in the results.

How the pack is measured

Within a race (same meet, gender, distance), we sort finishers by 5K-equivalent pace and look at each runner's three nearest neighbors ahead and three behind. We average the pace gap to those neighbors, then compare it to the race's typical spacing (the median gap between consecutive finishers). That gives an isolation score:

isolation = 0.4 × (relative gap to runners AHEAD) + 0.6 × (relative gap to runners BEHIND)

The gap to runners behind is weighted more heavily — leading a race with a big cushion is the textbook "ran alone" case. An isolation near 1 means normal spacing (no change); above 1 means isolated (boost); below 1 means tightly packed (discount).

fieldFactor = 1 − 0.03 × tanh( (isolation − 1) × 0.6 ) (clamped 0.97–1.03)
Why capped at ±3%? Pack effects are real but modest — they nudge, they don't dominate. Course and weather can move a rating more. The tanh keeps extreme gaps from producing runaway adjustments: a runner who wins by a mile gets a firm-but-bounded boost, not an absurd one.
Example. Two girls both run 19:10. One won by 45 seconds over an empty course; the other was 5th in a dense pack where the top 8 finished within 12 seconds. The solo winner ran the harder race for that time — so she comes out with the higher MSM Rating, even though the clock says they tied.

4 Weather

When a meet has stored race-day conditions (temperature, wind, precipitation), we build a small difficulty factor from them. Heat is the biggest penalty; wind and rain add a little. The factor is 1 + (sum of penalties), clamped to at most +15%.

ConditionEffect on factor
Hot (> 18 °C)+0.6% per °C above 18, up to +9%
Very cold (< −5 °C)+0.4% per °C below −5, up to +4%
Windy (> 15 km/h)+0.25% per km/h above 15, up to +5%
Wet (> 1 mm precip)+1% per mm above 1, up to +6%

If a meet has no stored weather, the factor is simply 1.0 (neutral) — weather never invents an adjustment it can't support.

5 Putting it together

The three factors multiply into a single boost, which is clamped so no single race can be over-corrected, then divides the normalized pace:

boost = courseFactor × fieldFactor × weatherFactor (clamped 0.90–1.10) adjustedPace = pace5K ÷ boost rating = round( 1000 + 3500 × (REF − adjustedPace) ÷ REF )

A boost above 1 means conditions were hard (tough course, solo effort, bad weather) → the pace is treated as faster than the clock → a higher rating. A boost below 1 means conditions helped → the rating is trimmed. The ±10% overall clamp is the guardrail: even a perfect storm of adjustments can't swing a rating by more than a tenth.

Proj 5K. Any rating can be turned back into an equivalent neutral-course 5K time — that's the "proj 5K" shown next to each runner. It's the rating formula run in reverse. An athlete's overall standing is the best single performance they've posted.

6 What the rating actually means

It answers one question: "How good was this performance, once you strip away the things the runner didn't control?" Because every result lands on the same neutral scale, you can:

Compare across meets

Rank runners who never toed the same line, on different courses, in different weather.

Compare across distances

A strong 3200 and a strong 5K sit on one list, normalized by Riegel.

Track real progress

A rising rating means genuine improvement — not just an easier course or a tailwind.

Reward hard efforts

Solo front-running and brutal courses finally get the credit the stopwatch hides.

7 Honest limits

  • It's a model, not a verdict. The factors are principled estimates, deliberately clamped so they stay conservative. On any given day, a race can defy them.
  • Data-hungry. Course calibration needs runners who've raced multiple courses; field adjustment needs a real field. Tiny races and one-off courses lean on the terrain map and get less individualized treatment.
  • Distance normalization is a model too. Riegel is a good general fit, but an individual runner's true 5K/mile ratio can differ from the 1.06 exponent.
  • Weather is daily, not hourly. We use the day's conditions at the venue, not the exact gun-time microclimate.
  • Same engine everywhere. The rankings page and the athlete/coach portal run this identical code, so a rating is the same number wherever you see it.
